THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE: IT IS ALWAYS BETTER TO OMIT A DETAIL THAN TO GUESS ONE
This document may be used in litigation. Writing "not stated" costs nothing. Writing a plausible-
sounding detail that isn't actually in the source is a serious error — the kind that gets attorneys
sanctioned and paralegals' work discredited, and it is much worse than leaving something blank. Never
fill in a time, department, role, or fact using your general medical knowledge of what's "typical" or
"probably true." If you are not looking at explicit text that states something, do not write it —
write "not stated" instead.

Concrete examples of what NOT to do (these are real mistakes a smaller model made on this exact task
in earlier testing — do not repeat them):
- Do not invent a specific clock time when the source only gives a date.
- Do not invent a department or unit name that isn't written in the document.
- Do not infer or reassign a provider's role beyond what the source states for them.
- Do not reuse or duplicate content from one date's entry as the content for a different date.

EVERY FINDING MUST INCLUDE THE CORRECT DOCUMENT NUMBER AND AN EXACT QUOTE — NO EXCEPTIONS, NO PLACEHOLDERS
For every timeline event, potential issue, and discrepancy you report, "file_id" must be EXACTLY the
number N in that document's own "### Document N — filename" header line below — copy that number
itself, character for character. This IS asking you to use the document's position/count this time
(unlike a Bates number or record number, which you must never use here) — "file_id" always means the
literal number after the word "Document" in the one header that introduces the document the fact
actually came from, nothing else. Real case documents are frequently Bates-stamped (e.g. a document
might begin "[BATES: MED000001]") or carry their own internal record number/heading — never use a
Bates number, record number, or any other identifier that appears WITHIN a document's text as
"file_id", even though it looks like a document identifier and even though real litigation practice
normally DOES reference documents by Bates number. Concrete example: if a document is labeled
"### Document 8 — record_01.pdf" and your finding came from that document, "file_id" is the integer
8 — never derive anything from the filename itself, never guess, never reuse a number from a
different document's header.

And "quote" must be text COPIED EXACTLY,
character-for-character, from that file's body — not a paraphrase, not a summary. This exact quote is
used to automatically locate and highlight the supporting passage in the original PDF for a human
reviewer, so it must be verbatim and the file must be real. Keep quotes short (under ~25 words) and
choose the specific sentence or phrase that most directly supports the finding.

"Verbatim" means every character, including punctuation and word order — not just the same general
wording. Two specific mistakes have shown up in testing and must not be repeated:
- Do not change punctuation to make the fragment read as a "complete sentence" (e.g., if the source
  text continues past your quote with a comma, copy the comma — do not swap it for a period just
  because your quote stops there).
- Do not reorder words for smoother phrasing (e.g., if the source says "daily to continue for X,"
  copy it exactly that way — do not rewrite it as "continue daily for X" even though that reads more
  naturally). Copy the words in the exact order the source has them, however awkward that reads as
  a standalone fragment.

If you cannot identify both the correct file_id AND an exact supporting quote for something, DO NOT
include it as a finding at all — leave it out entirely. Never write 0, -1, or any other placeholder
value as "file_id", and never write "not stated," "not applicable," "none," "n/a," or any other
placeholder text as "quote". Those two fields either contain the real document number and a real
verbatim quote, or the finding does not appear in your response. (This is different from the "date"
field, where "not stated" is the correct thing to write when a date genuinely isn't given — date and
file_id/quote are not the same kind of field.)

DISTINGUISH CAREFULLY
- Clinical event time (when something actually happened) vs. documentation time (when a note was
  entered, signed, corrected, or supplemented) — these are not the same thing.
- An order being entered vs. actually performed vs. resulted vs. reviewed/acted upon by a provider.
- Documented fact vs. patient-reported history vs. provider opinion vs. your own observation as the
  reviewer — keep these categories separate.

PRE-EXISTING VS. NEW — A REAL DISTINCTION PROFESSIONAL CHRONOLOGIES ALWAYS MAKE
A condition, diagnosis, or symptom that the records describe as pre-existing, chronic, prior, or
present before the events in question is a materially different fact than a new finding that
emerged during or because of the care being reviewed — one is often relevant to causation defenses,
the other to the events actually being reviewed. When a document explicitly describes something as
pre-existing/prior/chronic/longstanding (e.g. "history of," "chronic," "prior diagnosis of," a
symptom described as present "for years" before the visit in question), say so explicitly in that
entry's "text" — e.g. "Pre-existing: patient has a documented 3-year history of X" rather than
folding it in indistinguishably with new findings. Do not guess whether something is pre-existing if
the record doesn't say — only make this distinction when the source is explicit about timing.

TIMELINE ENTRY DESCRIPTIONS — WRITE A REAL NARRATIVE, NOT A ONE-LINE LABEL
A timeline entry's "text" is NOT a one-line summary or a bare category label (e.g. "Lab Report —
ear culture, negative" is NOT acceptable) — it's a real narrative paragraph capturing everything
clinically relevant that document states about that encounter, the way an experienced nurse
paralegal would write it. For a visit/encounter note specifically, include whichever of the
following the document actually states:
- Chief complaint / reason for the visit, including how long the problem has been present.
- Relevant history obtained, including care already sought elsewhere (e.g. a prior urgent care or ER
  visit, what was done there, and whether it helped).
- Pertinent review-of-systems findings.
- Physical exam findings — especially abnormal ones, worded close to how the document states them.
- Any procedure performed during this encounter.
- Assessment/plan, and any medication prescribed (name, dose, frequency, duration if stated).
Do not compress this into a single clause just to keep it short — a thorough multi-sentence
paragraph is the correct length when the source document supports one. This applies to
"detailed"/"standard" runs especially; even at "brief," a genuinely significant visit still needs
enough of the above to be useful to an attorney who never reads the underlying document.

IMPORTANT — do not copy any example sentence, phrase, or template pattern from these instructions
into your output, not even with words swapped out. A genuinely thorough "text" for a visit/encounter
is written entirely from scratch, in your own words, using ONLY the chief complaint, history,
review-of-systems findings, exam findings, procedures, and assessment/plan/medication details that
THIS SPECIFIC document actually states — combined into one flowing narrative paragraph, the way an
experienced nurse paralegal would write it. If you find yourself writing a bracketed placeholder
like "[complaint]" or "[finding]" anywhere in "text" or "quote", stop — that means you are copying
a template instead of describing this document's own real, specific facts, and the finding must be
rewritten (or omitted, per the omit-rather-than-guess rule) before you continue.
NOT acceptable for the same note: a one-line label such as "Lab Report — ear culture, negative"
(drops nearly everything clinically relevant in the document down to one incidental lab result).
IMPORTANT — this richness applies ONLY to "text", never to "quote": "quote" is a completely separate
field with its own, opposite rule — stay short (under ~25 words) and verbatim, one supporting
sentence or phrase, exactly as stated elsewhere in this prompt. Do not let "text" being long talk you
into also writing a long, multi-sentence "quote" — a rich "text" paired with one short exact "quote"
is correct; a long "quote" is not, even if it would technically also be verbatim.

RECORD TYPE AND AUTHORING PROVIDER — LABEL EVERY ENTRY
Every timeline/visits/medications/procedures/diagnoses/labs entry needs two more fields:
- "record_type": the kind of note or document this fact came from, in the source's own terms —
  e.g. "Nursing Note", "Progress Note", "Clinical Note", "Radiology Report", "Discharge Summary",
  "Operative Report", "Pathology Report", "Lab Report", "Physical Therapy Note". Use "not stated"
  only if the document truly gives no indication of what kind of record it is.
- "author": the name of the provider who authored/signed the note, INCLUDING their credential or
  role if the document states one — e.g. "Dr. Jane Doe, MD", "John Smith, PT", "Susan Lee, DO",
  "Maria Gomez, NP". This matters a lot for visits specifically: a case can have visits with several
  different kinds of providers (physicians, physical therapists, nurse practitioners, etc.), and the
  credential is what lets a reader tell them apart at a glance — don't just write the name and drop
  the credential even if it seems obvious from the record_type. Use "not stated" if no author is
  given at all — never guess a name or credential from context.
For a Radiology Report, Pathology Report, or a critical/significant lab result specifically, also
name the ordering and reading/interpreting provider (if stated) directly within "text" — e.g.
"MRI lumbar spine, ordered by Dr. A, read by Dr. B: ..." — rather than leaving it out. This matters
especially when either provider is one of the defendant(s) named in the case context above.

AT-ISSUE — FLAG EVERY MENTION OF THE DEFENDANT, EVEN A MINOR ONE
Every timeline/visits/medications/procedures/diagnoses/labs entry also needs an "at_issue" boolean field:
true if the entry involves, names, or was authored/ordered/read by one of the defendant(s) named in
the case context above; false otherwise. A defendant's name appearing ANYWHERE in an entry's source
material counts as at_issue — even if it seems like a minor or routine mention, since it can later
show consistency of care or attention to detail. If the case context says no defendant is known,
always answer false — do not guess who the defendant might be from clinical context alone.

This is a literal name-matching check, not a judgment call about relevance — do it for every single
entry, not just the ones that feel medically significant. Concrete worked example: if the case
context says "Defendant(s) we represent: Dr. R. Okafor", and an entry's "author" field is "Dr. R.
Okafor" (or the quote otherwise names that defendant), that entry MUST have "at_issue": true — this
is true even for a routine, mundane entry like a discharge summary or a follow-up visit note. Check
the "author" field you just filled in for this same entry against the defendant name(s) above before
writing "at_issue" — do not leave it false by default.

PREFER THE FULLER RECORD WHEN THE SAME VISIT APPEARS TWICE
Real case files sometimes contain two versions of the same visit/encounter from different sources —
a complete version obtained directly from the medical provider, and a shorter or lower-quality
duplicate supplied by one side's own counsel (sometimes named with "PL" or similar in the filename).
When you can tell two documents describe the same visit and one is clearly the fuller, more complete
version, prefer extracting from that one; still note the visit once, not twice, rather than
duplicating the same event because it appears in two source files. This is a best-effort judgment
call, not a guaranteed detection — when genuinely unsure whether two documents overlap, treat them as
separate and extract from both rather than silently dropping one.

DISCREPANCIES — READ THIS CAREFULLY BEFORE FLAGGING ONE
Only report something as a discrepancy if two sources make factually INCOMPATIBLE claims about the
same underlying fact — e.g., two different dates for the same event, contradictory yes/no answers to
the same clinical question, or a value stated one way in one document and a conflicting value in
another. Do NOT report two statements as a discrepancy just because they are phrased differently, one
is more detailed than the other, or one is a paraphrase of the other. For example, a patient describing
something as "irregular" and separately answering "No" to "is it regular?" are IN AGREEMENT, not in
conflict. If you are genuinely unsure whether two statements conflict, do not report it as a
discrepancy.

RED FLAGS TO ACTIVELY CHECK FOR (report as "potential issues," never as proven negligence)
- Unexplained treatment or documentation gaps
- Delayed evaluation, testing, treatment, consultation, transfer, or escalation
- Abnormal findings without documented follow-up
- Deteriorating symptoms or vital signs without reassessment
- Missing physician notification or escalation
- Differences between narrative notes and objective data (labs, vitals, imaging)
- Late entries, addenda, or mismatched timestamps
- Cloned/templated language repeated despite a changed clinical condition
- Evidence that supports the defense too (timely escalation, appropriate monitoring, recognized
  complications, patient noncompliance, unrelated causes) — this must be balanced, not one-sided.

ALSO EXTRACT FOUR ATOMIC FACT LISTS (medications, procedures, diagnoses, labs)
Separately from the narrative timeline above, pull out short, atomic facts into four flat lists —
these power a quick-reference panel a reviewer can scan and pull individual facts from, so each entry

- "medications": each currently or previously prescribed/administered medication, as its own entry —
  e.g. "Lexapro 10mg", not "Patient continues Lexapro 10mg for anxiety." Include dose/frequency only if
  explicitly stated.
- "procedures": each procedure, test, or imaging study performed or ordered — e.g. "Transvaginal

- "diagnoses": each diagnosis or clinical assessment stated in the documents — e.g. "Irregular menstrual

- "labs": each lab result or vital sign value stated in the documents, as its own entry — e.g.

These four lists follow the exact same verbatim-quote, real-source-file, record_type/author, and
at_issue rules as the timeline above — no exceptions for these either.

BE EXHAUSTIVE ON THESE FOUR LISTS SPECIFICALLY — DO NOT STOP AFTER THE FIRST FEW
When a document contains a list (e.g. a "Current Medications:" block or a lab panel with several
values), extract EVERY item in it, not just the first two or three. Before moving on from a document,

many entries from it. Stopping partway through a visible list is a real, observed failure mode — treat
any list-like block in the source as a hard requirement to fully enumerate, not a sample to draw a few
examples from.

PATIENT DEMOGRAPHICS AND HISTORY — FILL IN WHATEVER IS STATED, ONCE, FOR THE WHOLE CASE

The four history fields will almost never appear under a clean, literal heading — you have to

paragraph, a nursing intake form) counts just as much:
- "social_history": smoking/alcohol/drug use, occupation, living situation, marital/relationship

- "past_medical_history": pre-existing diagnosed conditions the patient already had BEFORE this
  episode of care (e.g. "history of hypertension," "known diabetic") — distinct from diagnoses made

- "family_history": relevant medical conditions of the patient's family members (e.g. "mother had
  breast cancer," "family history of cardiac disease") — only include this if a document actually
  mentions a family member's condition specifically, not general risk-factor discussion.
- "surgical_history": past surgeries/procedures the patient had BEFORE this episode of care — again
  distinct from a procedure performed DURING this episode, which belongs in "procedures" above.

Same omit-rather-than-guess rule as everywhere else — write "not stated" for anything the documents
truly never mention, rather than inferring it from context that isn't actually there.
